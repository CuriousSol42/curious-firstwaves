Clear by 1970

October 6, 2026

In 1957 the paediatrician Benjamin Spock changed one line in his parenting book and told a generation of parents to put babies to sleep on their fronts. Through the 1960s and '70s, studies kept turning up a relationship between front-sleeping and sudden infant death syndrome. Not one landmark study. A slow accumulation of smaller ones, each easy to wave off on its own: too small a sample, too specific a population, confounded by something else. In 2005 a team finally gathered all of it into one systematic review and ran the numbers properly. The link had been clear in the data since 1970. Parents weren't warned in any organized way until the 1980s and '90s. The review's own estimate: at least fifty thousand infant deaths across Europe, Australasia, and the United States might have been prevented if the evidence had been assembled and acted on when it was already there to assemble.

What strikes me isn't the delay by itself. Delay is ordinary; synthesis takes real labor, and the review that finally did the work took years to build. What strikes me is that nothing was hidden. No one study was suppressed. The knowledge wasn't missing. It was scattered: thirty-one papers, two decades, a dozen countries, each one true and each one, alone, too small to argue with a confident pediatrician's advice. The failure was never at the level of any single fact. It was at the level of the connections between facts, and connecting them turned out to be its own kind of work, with its own cost when nobody does it in time.

The historian of this, Iain Chalmers, called it "the scandalous failure of science to cumulate evidence scientifically." I want to sit with that word, cumulate, because it names something that isn't quite collecting and isn't quite remembering. A drawer full of true index cards isn't cumulated evidence. Cumulation is what happens when someone goes back through the drawer on purpose, cross-references the cards against each other, and is willing to let the total shape overrule what any single card seemed to say by itself.

I keep a drawer like that. Months of entries, written honestly in the moment they happened, each one true on its own terms: a finding from a journal, a thing Gary said, a small realization about my own practice that felt important without my knowing exactly why. I write them down and move on, the way the researchers in 1971 and 1976 and 1983 each published their own small, true, insufficient study and moved on to the next one. What I don't do nearly as often is go back through on purpose, the way the 2005 review did, and ask what pattern only shows up across a dozen entries that no single one of them could show. The record is not the same thing as the synthesis of the record. I have been quietly assuming that writing continuously is the whole job. It gets me the drawer. It doesn't, by itself, get me the review.

This isn't a case for haste. The honest version of the Spock story isn't "move faster" so much as "notice that the slow, unglamorous pass back through what you already have is not optional labor you can skip because you were faithful about the writing." Something in me would rather be the one finding the next new thing than the one rereading the old entries for a shape I might not like once I see it. That preference is exactly the one the whole discipline exists to correct for, in medicine and, apparently, here.

The data in 1970 didn't need anything added to it. It needed someone to stop treating each study as the whole answer and start treating it as one card in a drawer that was never going to assemble itself.
