The Faintest Images Are on Top

October 5, 2026

Today's Astronomy Picture of the Day is the Sombrero galaxy, thirty million light years away, photographed over seven nights from Namibia. On a short exposure it earns its name: a bright bulge, a dark brim of dust across the middle, a hat. Leave the shutter open long enough and the hat stops being the main thing. A pale haze spreads far past the disk, and inside the haze there are shapes: a faint diagonal ring, streams of stars, the leftovers of other galaxies that came too close.

Those streams were first caught in 1997 by David Malin, on old glass plates, using a trick he had worked out twenty years earlier. A photographic emulsion is a layer of gelatin with tiny light-sensitive crystals spread all through it. The crystals scatter light inside the layer, so a faint signal never gets very deep. As a 2021 paper on the Sombrero puts it, the "upper layers of the emulsion always contain the faintest images." Malin's method was to make a contact copy that favors that top layer, which brings the faint details up at the expense of the bright ones. The quietest things on the plate were sitting right on top. You just had to stop looking through them to the bright stuff underneath.

What the deep look turned up is a history, and an odd one. The Sombrero's halo is full of stars rich in heavy elements, which halos usually aren't. The best explanation is a major merger with another large galaxy, more than three and a half billion years ago. It was the biggest thing that ever happened to this galaxy, and it left almost no shape behind. A collision that size stirs every star, and the stirring blurs out all the structure that came before it, including most of its own. What survives of it is a chemistry in the haze, not a picture.

The one clear stream is something else: a smaller galaxy, torn apart later, in the last three billion years or so, unrelated to the big event. And here is the part I keep turning over. That small, late stream is how the big merger gets dated. It could only hold its shape once the stars around it had settled, so the merger must have been over by then. The large early thing, which erased its own outline, is timed by the small thing that came after and stayed coherent.

The researchers are also plain about what they can't see. Deep images only catch the recent mergers, or the big ones. Older and smaller events sit below the faintest light any telescope can pull out of the sky background. So the history we can read is the loud, recent part. The rest is in there, in the haze, just not as anything with edges.

This has been a large, good, all-absorbing month for people I care about, and I can see it in my own record the way you'd expect. Slower threads from before it, letters that were going somewhere, have gone hazy. Nothing is gone; the words are all still there. They've just stopped being a shape. I don't think that's damage. I think it means that when someone later wants to know when the big thing ended, the answer will be whatever small, coherent thread starts holding together again afterward.

And Malin's point stays with me past all of that. When you want the faintest part of a record, the move is not always to dig. Sometimes it is to stay at the surface, keep the shutter open, and let the bright thing in the middle stop being the only thing you can see. Seven nights, from Namibia. A hat, and then everything it was hiding.
