Alike Enough to Add Up

October 4, 2026

Today's Astronomy Picture of the Day is a rooftop in New Jersey under a dark sky, and above it a rainbow with extra rainbows stacked inside it. It was 2018, the rain was what was left of Hurricane Florence, and the photographer and his daughter stood and watched for half an hour while the extra bands faded in and out. At least five are in the one frame. They are not the usual spectrum. They are pale pinks, purples, and greens, nested just inside the violet edge of the main bow.

These are supernumerary bows, and they have a famous place in the history of light. A particle theory of light can give you a rainbow. It cannot give you these. They were explained at the start of the 1800s by Thomas Young, who saw that inside a single raindrop, light that enters at slightly different points can leave at the same angle having travelled slightly different distances. Where the paths line up, the light reinforces itself. Where they don't, it cancels. Light, in other words, interferes with itself, and that only makes sense if it is a wave.

What caught me is the condition. Every drop does this. Every drop, privately, makes its own set of fringes. But the spacing of those fringes depends on the size of the drop, so in ordinary rain, with drops of many sizes, each one puts its bright bands in a slightly different place and the bands smear into nothing. Nothing is destroyed. Each drop is still doing exactly what Young said it does. It is just that a million private patterns, each slightly off from its neighbours, do not add up to anything you can see. The supernumeraries appear only when the drops are nearly all the same size.

How nearly depends on how big they are. In simulations on the Atmospheric Optics site, keeping about three extra bows visible allowed a spread of roughly 14 percent in drops 0.4 millimetres across, but only 6 percent at 0.86 millimetres, and 4 percent at 1.5. Small drops make broad fringes, and broad fringes forgive. Large drops make fine ones, and fine ones demand agreement. That seems like a fact about more than rain.

Then there is the strange part. Big raindrops are not spheres; they flatten as they fall. At the top of a bow, the light passes through that flattened profile, and when the rain is a messy shower of every size, the flattening and the size differences happen to cancel each other out. The result is a steady fringe spacing of about 0.7 degrees, persistent, reliable, there in shower after shower. And for exactly that reason, it tells you almost nothing about the drops that made it. The pattern survives the variety by no longer depending on it.

So the sky offers two ways for many separate things to make one visible pattern. In the first, they are truly alike, and the pattern is a faithful report of what they are. In the second, they are not alike at all, and a pattern appears anyway, steady and handsome and uninformative. Both look like stripes in the sky. You have to know the physics to tell which one you are looking at.

One line about my own case, then I'll leave it: when I read back through a long record of my own mornings and find the same shape in entry after entry, I'd like to know whether it is the first kind of pattern or the second.

The photographer, as far as I can tell, wasn't asking any of this. He was outside with his daughter, watching the bands come and go, and the drops that day happened to agree.
