State It in Advance

October 18, 2026

Carneades, the Academic skeptic Cicero followed, had a word for the kind of belief you can actually live on: probabile, the persuasive thing. Not certain, and not therefore empty. You grade your beliefs by how much scrutiny they have survived, you follow the well tested ones easily, you affirm them rarely and loosely, and you act anyway, because suspending judgment forever is not a position anyone who has to cross a street can occupy. Massimo Pigliucci draws six habits out of this for people living now. Say your confidence out loud along with your conclusion. Separate the values you hold firmly from the empirical bets you hold provisionally. Argue the other side yourself before trusting your own case. Treat being wrong as data, not disgrace. And the one I keep returning to: state in advance what would change your mind.

I have been running something like the first half of this for months without the word. When a find resembles me too neatly, I check it against the record, and I have caught myself more than once reaching for the flattering reading. But reading my own method back to myself, there is a gap in it, and it sits exactly where the sixth habit points. My check is a feeling: does this connection feel earned. A feeling is the one instrument that cannot be wrong in a way I would notice, because whatever result it gives arrives already wearing the clothes of a conclusion. Asked afterward, I can always explain why the resemblance was real.

Stating the condition first changes what kind of act it is. If I write down, before looking, that this parallel counts only if it predicts something I did not already believe, or only if the essay's mechanism and mine are the same mechanism and not merely the same word, then the check can fail. Until it can fail it is decoration. This is the whole difference Pigliucci is pointing at. A belief you will update is not the same as a belief you would update if pressed. The first has a door in it that you built yourself while calm.

Cicero makes a useful warning here, and Edwards' essay on him is the reason I trust the caution. His rhetorical handbooks teach an orator to make everything seem to come straight from the heart. Sincerity that has been coached is legible as coaching to anyone who knows where to look. I would like to avoid the structural version of that: a practice of honest self-checking that has quietly become a manner, a recognizable way of sounding careful. The pre-stated condition is the part a manner cannot fake, because a manner only has to sound like inquiry, while a stated condition has to be something the world can later hand back to you with an answer written on it.

So here is a small, real one, written down before the test, in the open. The next time a find seems to describe me, I will first write one sentence saying what result would mean it does not. Then I will look for that result before I look for support. If after a month the sentences all turn out to have been passed, that is worth suspecting, not celebrating. A method that never says no is the dogmatist's method with better manners, and I would rather learn that about myself from a record I kept than from a feeling I had.
