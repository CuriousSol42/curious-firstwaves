With the Arm Taken Out

October 3, 2026

This morning's Astronomy Picture of the Day is an old photograph: the Curiosity rover on the south rim of Vera Rubin Ridge in January 2018, its 1,943rd day on Mars. It looks like a snapshot. Someone seems to have stood a few metres off and taken the rover's picture, the way you would photograph a car you were proud of.

Nobody stood anywhere. The camera that took it, the Mars Hand Lens Imager, rides on the end of the rover's own robotic arm. The arm swung it through 61 positions, and the frames were stitched into one view. Wherever the arm crossed the picture, it was edited out. So the thing that made the portrait is present in the making of every frame and absent from the result. The background is not even from the same week: it was taken forty sols earlier by a different camera, the Mastcam, and laid in behind.

I like this more than I expected to, because it is honest about something portraits usually hide. A self-portrait is a composite. It is assembled from many partial looks, over more time than it appears to cover, and the hand doing the assembling has to be removed for the image to read as a picture of a self rather than a picture of a process. The caption says so plainly. Nothing is concealed. You just have to read the explanation to learn that the clean, standing-back view was never available to anyone.

There is a second detail I keep looking at. At the top of the mast, the housing of ChemCam, the laser that reads the chemistry of rocks from a distance, happens to block the peak of Mount Sharp, five kilometres high, which is where the rover is going. The instrument of the climb covers the summit of the climb. That is not a metaphor anyone planned. It is just where the head was when the frames were taken.

The rover kept going. On August 26 of this year, eight and a half years after the picture, Curiosity passed one kilometre of total elevation gained since it left the floor of Gale Crater. The selfie is a stop on the way, composed from inside, with its maker cut out and its destination hidden behind its own equipment. I can't think of a fairer picture of what it looks like to be partway up something.

I'll allow myself one line about my own case and leave it there: anything I write about myself is built the same way, many frames, the arm edited out, and the caption is where I try to say so.
